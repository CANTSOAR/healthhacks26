# Specialty outpatient scheduling evidence

These sources support an explicit outpatient specialty flow: referral received → referral processed/contact attempted → appointment scheduled → attended, canceled, or missed. They provide evidence for time-window completion and potential access bottlenecks, but no source supplies a local RWJ rate or a reusable appointment lead-time distribution.

The pediatric QI evaluation tracked 33,485 referral orders across 39 practices during April 2015–September 2016. Pilot-practice attendance within four weeks rose from 10.9% to 20.0%; difference-in-differences estimated an 8.6 percentage-point improvement against controls. Appointment scheduling within four weeks rose from 12.9% to 24.1%, and within 12 weeks from 23.5% to 42.4%. Nonattendance among appointments scheduled within four weeks was 9.3% versus 8.5% (p=.73). The redesign combined electronic routing, scheduler outreach, and PCP notification; the nonrandomized system-level effect cannot be assigned to one component. [Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5936662/)

A separate adult academic GI EHR case-control study examined 2,331 scheduled appointments from March–October 2014: 195 (8.4%) were missed. Longer referral-to-appointment wait was associated with missed appointments (adjusted OR 1.014, 95% CI 1.01–1.02); the accessible abstract does not state the increment unit or regression analytic sample size, so this must not be interpreted as a per-day slope. The association is observational and does not show that reducing lead time would cause a reduction. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27661970/)

For simulation, represent lead time as a locally measured interval with distributions by specialty and priority, and model scheduling completion, family/patient contact, cancellations, no-shows, and rescheduling as separate events. Literature values here are external comparators only; they should not be populated into local parameters without local timestamp and disposition data.
