# Literature-grounded interaction variations

These papers provide qualitative and observational examples for optional synthetic scenario variations. They do not describe Robert Wood Johnson University Hospital (RWJUH), its personnel, current local workflows, or local frequencies. Findings below are observations in the named study settings; proposed scenario uses are inferences for simulation design only. They should not be treated as prevalence estimates, clinical rules, or claims about any real staff member. No treatment or dosing behavior is specified here.

## Evidence and bounded scenario ideas

| Study and observed setting | What the researchers observed | Optional scenario variation (inference only) |
|---|---|---|
| Maughan, Lei & Cydulka (2011), one major metropolitan teaching-hospital ED, 8 weeks; 110 handoff sessions / 992 patients. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2009.12.004), [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20825820/), Results in abstract. | Examination findings were reported differently or omitted in 130 (13.1%) and 447 (45.1%) of 992 patient handoffs; laboratory findings in 37 (3.7%) and 290 (29.2%). Errors/omissions were defined against physician documentation. Written/electronic support was associated with fewer examination omissions and electronic support with fewer laboratory errors. | Give learner a handoff and chart with one documented finding omitted or contradicted, then let them notice and clarify it. Keep discrepancy synthetic and chart-grounded; do not imply the measured rates apply locally. |
| Redley et al. (2017), two acute hospital EDs in Victoria, Australia; 66 observed shift-change handovers and focus groups with 34 nurses. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aenj.2017.05.003), [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28595847/), Results in abstract. | The authors organized handover into antecedents, behaviours/interactions, content, and delegation of ongoing care. Infrequent/ad hoc interprofessional communication and discipline-specific handover processes/content emerged as risks; themes included standard processes, teamwork/interactions, and communication practices. | Vary whether the learner explicitly confirms who owns follow-up and whether another discipline has shared its relevant update. Keep the handoff context realistic but do not prescribe a universal script from this qualitative study. |
| Weigl et al. (2017), one large academic ED in Augsburg, Germany; 77 matched 90-minute observations and staff self-ratings across 20 randomly selected days (80 sessions scheduled). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-019074), [open full text](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/7/12/e019074), Results, Figures 1–2 and Methods. | Authors defined interruptions as unexpected task/communication causing the focal task to stop and an observable task switch. They reported 877 observed interruptions over 116 h 51 min (7.51/hour); common sources were cross-profession colleagues (238), same-profession colleagues (211), and phone/beeper (184). Of 791 classifiable contents, parallel cases (240), current case (151), and coordination (144) were most common. Parallel-case interruptions were associated with higher reported work stress; most interruption types were not. | Introduce a brief interruption about a parallel patient or coordination during a synthetic task, then assess whether learner marks a place, resumes, and rechecks relevant information. Do not represent 7.51/hour as a general ED rate: one German site, sampled observation sessions, and a paper-specific task-switch definition. |
| Aranguri, Davidson & Ramirez (2006), primary-care offices in four US regions; 16 patients with 9 physicians, 13 interpreted Spanish-English visits and 3 Spanish visits with one bilingual physician. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1525-1497.2006.00451.x), [full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1525-1497.2006.00451.x), Abstract, Methods, Results and “Questions Asked by Patients.” | Transcript analysis found interpreter-mediated speech reduced/revised; all studied visits showed examples of content changes. The Results section reports 28 direct patient questions in 13 interpreted visits, 8 answered directly by the interpreter and not conveyed to the physician. The same passage says this averaged 1.75 questions per visit, which conflicts with 28/13 (about 2.15); the paper's count and stated mean are internally inconsistent. Interpreted visits also had little rapport-building small talk. | In an optional communication variation, have the learner address the patient directly, pause for turn-by-turn interpretation, and confirm that a patient question reaches the clinician and is answered in the appropriate role. This is a primary-care, small, selected sample; it does not establish how professional interpreters or ED encounters perform. |

## Use and transfer limits

Use these as prompts for variation in attention, handoff completeness, role clarity, and patient voice—not as local workforce calibration. Their sites, dates, definitions, samples, participant mix, and care settings differ. A scenario author may select one event pattern at a time and label it as synthetic. Any local claim about RWJUH workflow, staffing, handoff norms, language-service availability, or rates requires local evidence and review. Observed associations do not prove causality or patient harm. These papers do not validate a clinical decision, treatment, or educational outcome.
