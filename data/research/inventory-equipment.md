# Hospital equipment inventory and operations research

This source set adds a generic equipment-management layer for hospital workflow modeling. It contains 13 records: shared asset-lifecycle and maintenance frameworks, plus candidate equipment-family workflows for ventilators, patient monitors, infusion pumps, defibrillators, anesthesia systems, ultrasound, CT/MRI, dialysis, central sterilization, and blood-bank refrigeration. Only the WHO maintenance appendix examples explicitly name ECG monitors, infusion pumps, anesthesia units and X-ray systems. Other family labels are analyst-selected candidates mapped to general lifecycle guidance; cited sources do not establish that those assets exist at RWJ or give their model-specific instructions.

The records do not describe RWJ's actual assets, models, quantities, condition, staffing, service contracts, use rates, maintenance intervals, or throughput. The named device families are generic candidates for local inventory reconciliation. Data fields and process links suggested in `inferred_scenario_use` are modeling recommendations, not observations. No prices, durations, capacities, numeric maintenance intervals, or clinical settings are fabricated.

## Source interpretation and limits

Primary sources inspected on 2026-10-10:

- WHO, [Inventory and maintenance management information system for medical device](https://www.who.int/publications/b/74278) (2025): explicitly describes the medical-device lifecycle through incoming inspection, inventory, safe-use training, maintenance and decommissioning.
- WHO, [Introduction to medical equipment inventory management](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789241501392) (2011): describes maintaining current inventories, updating them when equipment arrives or is retired and during audits, risk-based inventory detail, and related consumables, spare parts and testing/safety tools.
- WHO, [Medical equipment maintenance programme overview](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241501538) (2011): outlines performance and safety inspection, preventive and corrective maintenance; Appendix B gives example procedures for an anesthesia/analgesia unit, ECG monitor, infusion pump and X-ray system. These examples do not establish local asset presence.
- CDC, [Environmental Cleaning Procedures](https://www.cdc.gov/healthcare-associated-infections/hcp/cleaning-global/procedures.html): calls for manufacturer cleaning instructions, compatible products, staff training and written schedules with method, frequency and owner; includes dialysis station cleaning workflows.
- CDC, [Sterilizing Practices](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/disinfection-sterilization/sterilizing-practices.html): discusses sterilizer monitoring records, load records and response to positive biological indicators.
- CDC, [Recommendations for Disinfection and Sterilization](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/disinfection-sterilization/summary-recommendations.html): gives risk-based cleaning, disinfection and sterilization requirements and examples such as respiratory equipment, anesthesia circuits and endocavitary probes.

WHO publications establish equipment-management processes; CDC establishes selected infection-prevention/reprocessing constraints. Neither source set supplies a facility-specific equipment census. Most family-specific candidate fields in this catalog are explicit inferences from those general controls. They should remain uncalibrated until matched to the target hospital's inventory, manufacturer instructions, biomedical-engineering records, infection-prevention policy, training records, and service-line workflows. The sources do not authorize substituting generic product instructions for the installed model's validated procedures.
