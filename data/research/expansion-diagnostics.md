# Diagnostic and medication workflow evidence for hospital expansion

These nine extracts are external-study observations intended as candidates for later scenario assumptions. None measures Rutgers/New Brunswick operations or establishes local staffing, capacity, service time, or patient mix. Keep service intervals separate: laboratory analytic/processing time, draw-to-result time, imaging report turnaround, pharmacy dispensary processing, and total encounter/medication time have different starts and endpoints.

## Laboratory: point-of-care versus central testing

Chaisirin et al. conducted a randomized study in 248 adult Siriraj Hospital urgency-room patients (124 per arm) who required electrolyte testing, from April to October 2017. Table 3 reports median blood-draw-to-all-results intervals of 72 minutes (IQR 54.5–90) for POCT and 87.5 minutes (IQR 70–103) for central lab testing. The same table has a separate “Laboratory turnaround time” row: POCT 5 minutes (IQR 4–6), CLT 87.5 minutes (IQR 70–103). The paper defines lab TAT as sample access to result reporting. Keep that row distinct from blood-draw-to-all-results. The CLT median is 87.5 in both rows; only the lab-TAT row gives the 70–103 IQR for CLT. The randomized study also found arrival-to-physician-decision medians of 106.5 minutes (IQR 78.25–140) versus 204.5 (IQR 165–244), but that is a composite endpoint and cannot be assigned wholly to lab turnaround. This was a single-center selected cohort; it is a comparator, not a local forecast. [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7081845/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2019.10.43655)

## Imaging and radiology reporting

At Haaglanden Medical Center Westeinde, an observational before/after study compared 9-month periods (December 2016–September 2017 and December 2017–September 2018, per Methods). For overall 24/7 radiology orders, Table 8 reports median report turnaround falling from 91 minutes (IQR 45–256; 20,082 orders) to 50 (IQR 30–106; 20,687 orders). CT medians were 122 (IQR 61–413) and 67 (42–142) minutes; plain radiograph medians were 69 (36–195) and 41 (25–83). The Table 8 period counts sum to 40,769 orders (20,082 + 20,687); the article states 99 turnaround values were missing, leaving 40,670 orders with nonmissing turnaround data. The bundle included added emergency nursing and specialist coverage, Lean work on radiology, and extended admission-office hours; it is not possible to isolate a single intervention effect. The site excluded patients redirected to a neighboring general-practitioner cooperative. These observed values are external comparators, not local turnaround targets. [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6712614/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12245-019-0238-7)

## Pharmacy order processing

Naylor et al. retrospectively audited a 231-bed Winnipeg community hospital's inpatient pharmacy during normal operating hours (07:00–21:00). The baseline audit covered May 2009; the post-education audit covered 28 randomly selected days from August 17 to October 9, 2009. Table 2 reports dispensary turnaround (prescribing to medication leaving the dispensary) separately from total turnaround (prescribing to administration). Median dispensary times for stat/emergent orders (n=30, 37) were 10 and 9 minutes; urgent/now orders (n=54, 105) were 10 and 12 minutes. Under-15-minute dispensary completion was reported as 28/30 (90%) and 35/37 (95%) for stat/emergent, and 48/54 (89%) and 94/105 (89%) for urgent/now. The baseline stat/emergent count and printed percentage conflict: 28/30 calculates to 93.3%, while Table 2 prints 90%; the extract retains both as reported and calculated. The definitions/education intervention did not significantly change the medians. This does not cover continuous 24-hour processing or delivery-to-bed time and should not be confused with medication administration. [Full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3203827/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4212/cjhp.v64i5.1070)

## Transfer and interpretation

For a future local simulator, these studies can support sensitivity ranges for distinctly modeled laboratory, imaging-report, and pharmacy-dispatch events. A user may select an external source distribution as an explicit assumption, but it must remain marked as inferred and carry its cited site, cohort, study period, units, denominator, and endpoint. Do not transfer study averages to local patient-level truth, infer active clinician labor from elapsed turnaround, or treat a bundled before/after contrast as a causal effect of one component. Replace proxy values when local workflow observations become available and approved for use.

## Search and source checks

- Full-text XML for the laboratory RCT and radiology study was retrieved from Europe PMC and inspected at the cited verification URLs. For pharmacy, Europe PMC fullTextXML returned HTTP 500; the full article page with `?report=xml` was inspected instead and provided the methods, audit dates, denominators, and Table 2. The pharmacy table reports baseline stat/emergent <15-minute count 28/30 as 90%; arithmetic yields 93.3%, so both are preserved with the discrepancy labeled.
- Excluded leads: an ED CT quality-improvement article reported order-to-final-report times, but the selected radiology study supplied richer modality-specific 24/7 and peak-hour distributions; a CPOE pharmacy comparison focused on order-to-pharmacist verification rather than a dispensary departure endpoint, so the chosen pharmacy audit better documents the pharmacy workflow stage and sample denominators. These are source-selection notes, not claims that the excluded studies are invalid.
